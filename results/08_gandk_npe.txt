Signed versus clipped masses: where the shape error actually comes from
====================================================================================
  fraction of negative masses      0.462
  ESS / n_atoms after clipping     0.174   (1.0 would be the prior itself)

  posterior standard deviation, averaged over the scored observations:
            ABC  NCP signed  NCP clip  NCP iso      NPE   prior
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
     A    0.804       0.623     2.410    1.043    0.626   2.887
     B    2.122       1.903     2.698    2.108    1.874   2.886
     g    2.577       2.510     2.827    2.537    2.495   2.888
     k    0.972       0.832     2.336    1.126    0.887   2.887
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The signed column is the estimator of Eq. (2) verbatim; the clip and iso
  columns are two ways of turning the same numbers into a probability measure.
  If signed tracks the ABC while clipped runs towards the prior, the negative
  mass is not numerical noise to be discarded: it is carrying the information
  that makes the posterior tighter than the prior, and clipping throws it out.
  The isotonic projection keeps it -- it accumulates the signed masses into a
  CDF and projects THAT onto monotone functions, so a negative mass still
  pulls probability off the atoms before it instead of being deleted.

Cost of one more functional, after training
====================================================================================
  300 observations, NPE using 4000 draws each.

         cold (ms/obs)   warm (ms/obs)    f evaluated at
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   NCP          0.0374          0.0031       20,000 pts
   NPE          0.2401          0.0040    1,200,000 pts
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  cold: 6x in NCP's favour, warm: 1x
  The structural reason is the last column. The operator's atoms are the same
  stored prior draws for every observation, so a new f is evaluated once for
  the whole batch and each observation costs one dot product. NPE's draws are
  per-observation, so f must be evaluated n_obs x n_draws times and the count
  grows with the batch. The gap widens with the number of observations queried,
  not with the number of functionals.

Reading
====================================================================================
  Read the W1 column, not the mean column. Under this prior g is only weakly
  identified by 100 observations, so a method can land the posterior mean and
  still be badly wrong about the shape; W1 against the reference catches that
  and the mean error does not.

  On the clipped measure -- what every order-statistic query uses by default --
  NPE is several times better on shape, and the rank sweep at the top of this
  file rules out capacity as the explanation. But the posterior MEANS tie, and
  the signed standard deviations tie, which says the operator has estimated the
  dependence perfectly well and is losing the information afterwards, in the
  step that makes the masses non-negative. Swapping clipping for the monotone
  projection closes essentially the whole gap. So the finding is not 'NPE beats
  the operator on this model'; it is 'clip-and-renormalise is the wrong
  projection, and it was costing the operator the comparison'.

  Everything here is scored against an ABC reference, which is itself an
  estimate. The 'ref floor' column says how far that reference moves when its
  own tolerance is tightened fourfold. A method-to-method gap smaller than the
  floor is not evidence of anything -- which is why the iso-versus-NPE
  difference should be read as a tie rather than as a win for either.
