====================================================================================
  Every method beats the prior-only baseline on every row, so the epidemic
  curve is informative about (beta, gamma) and all three find that signal.

  NPE is the most accurate here, on every functional. That is the expected
  result rather than a surprise: this posterior is smooth, unimodal and
  two-dimensional, which is exactly what a conditional mixture density models
  well. Direct regression is close behind on the functionals it can target,
  as it should be -- it optimises for that one functional and nothing else.

  The operator is the least accurate of the three, and one row is much worse
  than the others: the 0.9 quantile of R0. It still beats prior-only, but only
  by about a factor of two, against NPE's factor of sixty. This is the
  over-dispersion of the low-rank posterior showing up where it hurts most --
  a tail quantile, at 14x posterior concentration. Moments and probabilities
  are within a factor of three of the best method; tail order statistics are
  not, and should not be reported from a low-rank fit at this concentration
  without raising the rank a great deal or falling back to sampling.

  What the operator wins on is the shape of the cost, decisively: one fit, and
  then any functional -- including nonlinear ones like R0 -- as a weighted
  average over stored draws, with no sampling and no refit. So the honest
  claim is a cost claim, not an accuracy claim. When a single functional is
  known in advance, regress on it directly; when the full density is wanted
  and is well behaved, NPE is stronger; the operator is for the case where
  many low-order functionals will be asked of one fit, cheaply.
